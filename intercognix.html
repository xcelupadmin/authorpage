Beyond the Book

Intercognix

The unknown between the known — a word, and a practice, for discoveries hiding between things we already know.

The Idea

A library without corridors

Picture everything humanity has written as one vast library — medicine in one wing, physics in another, history, law and literature each in its own hall. What the library lacks is corridors. A historian rarely reads a pharmacology paper; a physicist rarely meets an idea buried in ancient poetry.

So some of what we most need to know may already be implied by what we know — not inside any single hall, but between them. There was no word for that kind of finding, so Jay Gontu coined one.

intercognixn.

A candidate insight discoverable through previously unrecognized relationships among independently known things.

inter, between · cogni, knowing · x, the unknown

The Process

Ordinary search is built to find what is relevant. Intercognix looks instead for productive surprise. Every candidate is worked through the same four steps, and none is accepted on the strength of a good story.

Step One

Find the Unexpected Proximity

Look for concepts from maximally distant disciplines that turn out to sit close together in meaning — the kind of closeness advanced language models can now surface at a scale no single reader could manage.

Step Two

Name the Bridge

Set the obvious resemblance aside and ask what underlying principle could explain it. That principle is the bridge — never stored in either field, yet inferable the moment both are held in the same frame.

Step Three

Ask the New Question

State the question that becomes possible only when the two ideas are considered together — a question neither discipline would think to ask on its own.

Step Four

Name the Test That Could Break It

Specify what evidence would support the candidate and, just as importantly, what would falsify it. A candidate that cannot be broken is merely poetry.

Most candidates should collapse under evidence and expert scrutiny. The few that survive become discoveries nobody was looking for.

From the Book to the Corridors

Close Your Eyes to See asks how civilizations preserve knowledge across fragile generations — and how much of it goes unseen simply because we no longer know how to look. Intercognix carries the same question into the present. We now hold more recorded knowledge than any civilization before us, and we still read it one hall at a time.

The two are, in the end, one discipline: patient attention to what is already in front of us, and a willingness to sit with questions that do not resolve quickly.
